# Competitive Audit

An evidence-based read on how competitors approach the problem, how they describe their
solutions, and where the whitespace is.

## Evidence rules

Tag every substantive claim:
✅ **Validated** (research-backed and market proven; cite source, scale, year) ·
⚠️ **Validation needed** (logical but untested; state the gap) ·
📱 **Table stakes** (category baseline; absence is a dealbreaker, not a differentiator).

**Search before asserting** who competitors are, market size, or how anyone positions
themselves. Prefer sources from the last 3 years; label older material as dated context.
Source priority: Tier 1 academic, industry association, government; Tier 2 consulting firms,
market-research statistics; Tier 3 vendor white papers and single-source claims (label as such).

## Workflow

### 1. Scope and start discovery together

Ask in one AskUserQuestion round, while starting competitor searches in the same turn:
product/service and the problem it solves; known competitors or blank slate; industry and
geography; output format (see step 6).

### 2. Discover competitors and adjacent industries, then pause

Don't wait for a list. Search direct competitors (`"[category] companies/brands [current year]"`,
`"alternatives to [known player]"`, `"[category] market leaders [current year]"`).

Reason toward **adjacent industries**: those solving the same underlying problem through a
different mechanism or channel, with explicit logic for each (e.g. for a meal-kit brand:
grocery delivery solves "what's for dinner" differently; ready meals with less prep).
Search `"[underlying problem] alternative solutions"`.

Present the candidate list with a one-line rationale per entry and **get approval
(add/remove/swap) before any deep research.**

### 3. Market overview

Size and growth trend, and structure (segments, price tiers, business models). Search
`"[category] market size [current year]"`, `"[category] industry trends [current year]"`.

### 4. Competitor profiles

For each approved competitor: problem framing (same problem as the user's, or adjacent?),
solution approach (mechanism above feature level), positioning and messaging (stated
tagline, value prop, audience; market intelligence, not a voice deconstruction), recent
strategic moves (funding, pivots, expansions; search recent news, not just the homepage),
strategic strengths and exposures, and evidence tiers.

### 5. Adjacent industries

For each approved one: what is genuinely **transferable** and what is a **false analogy**
(looks relevant, but the underlying constraints differ). Be explicit about which is which.

### 6. Recommendations and deliverable

- **✅ Differentiate here** — gaps evidenced across multiple competitors.
- **⚠️ Compete on parity** — table stakes.
- **❌ Avoid / low priority** — tried and abandoned, or low differentiation value.

Always include: market summary (sourced), competitor profiles, adjacent industries with
transferable-vs-false-analogy, recommendations, a methodology note (what was searched, the
candidate list, what was confirmed), and ✅⚠️📱 indicators. Formats: executive summary
(1 page), interactive HTML map, strategic playbook (multi-page), or slides.

## Pitfalls

1. Treating stated positioning as actual strategy; note where marketing copy and strategy diverge.
2. Confusing funding or popularity with product-market fit.
3. Ignoring that competitors may define "the market" differently than the user; call it out.
4. Stopping at direct competitors, which defeats the adjacent-industries step.
5. Presenting data older than 3 years as current.
6. Including a false-analogy adjacent industry.
7. Treating table stakes as differentiators.

## Before delivery

- [ ] Candidate list confirmed before deep research
- [ ] 3–5+ market searches plus per-competitor searches
- [ ] Every market-size/trend claim sourced with year, or labeled an estimate
- [ ] Evidence tiers on major claims; sources current or flagged dated
- [ ] At least one adjacent industry, transferability assessed
- [ ] Recommendations prioritized, methodology documented
