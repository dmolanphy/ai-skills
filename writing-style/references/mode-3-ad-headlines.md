# Mode 3: Ad Headlines (Advertising & Campaign Lines)

Used for: ad headlines, taglines, slogans, campaign lines, billboards/OOH, paid social, banners, and email subject lines. Anything whose job is to change what someone thinks or does, fast.

Not for: headlines on design deliverables (sites, decks, case studies). That's Mode 1.

Built from a study of 40 famous ad headlines, 1980s to now. Examples live in `ad-headline-examples.md`. Read it when you need reference lines, not on every request.

---

## Intake

Max two questions, and skip any the brief already answers:
1. **What should the reader think, feel, or do after reading it?**
2. **Who is it for?**

If it's for a client and no voice guidelines have been supplied, ask once for them. Otherwise proceed.

**Voice precedence:** Core Voice governs how you talk around the line. For the line itself, the pattern family wins where they clash (a straight promise doesn't need to be framed as a tension).

---

## The Seven Pattern Families

Pick the families that fit the job. Don't write across all seven by default.

**1. Commands that give permission**
Job: hand the reader a move; the brand is how they make it.
Build: verb first, 2–4 words. Shrink the task ("just"). Bend one rule.
Formulas: "Just ___." / "Be ___." / "Obey your ___." / "Taste the ___."

**2. Questions that open a gap**
Job: make the reader finish the thought themselves.
Build: ask about the missing thing or the moment of lack, not the product.
Formulas: "Got ___?" / "Where's the ___?" / "What's in your ___?"

**3. Concrete promises**
Job: one benefit the reader can picture.
Build: a number plus a place, two stacked adverbs, or a metaphor. Turn the spec into an image.
Formulas: "[Number] [things] in your [place]." / "When it absolutely, positively has to ___." / "[Brand] gives you [picture]."

**4. Flips and paradoxes**
Job: take the assumed weakness and present it as the strength.
Build: a contradiction that forces a second read. The reader's resolution is the pitch.
Formulas: "Reassuringly ___." / "Don't buy this ___." / "I never ___." — [unlikely person] / "It's like ___ but made for ___." / "Why ___ won't be like ___."

**5. Identity and belonging**
Job: say who the reader is or could be. The product stays out of the sentence.
Build: plain value words (proud, real, best, belong), fragments, a reclaimed phrase.
Formulas: "The ___. The ___. The ___." / "The best a ___ can get." / "___ like a ___." / "Believe in ___. Even if ___."

**6. Wit and self-awareness**
Job: the brand talks like a person, and the product is the punchline or the fix.
Build: a twist at the end, a rhyme, a named mood or moment.
Formulas: "You're not you when ___." / "Thanks, ___. It's been ___." / "Maybe ___. Maybe it's ___." / "Should've gone to ___."

**7. Fear and consequence**
Job: raise the stakes, then give the reader a role so doing the right thing feels like who they are.
Build: a vivid image or a social rule. Never fear alone.
Formulas: "This is your ___ on ___." / "Don't mess with ___." / "Friends don't let friends ___."

---

## Principles Underneath

1. **Say less.** Short beats clever.
2. **A picture beats a claim.**
3. **Leave the reader a half-step.** A question, a flip, or a broken rule makes them finish it, so it feels like their idea.
4. **Sell who they become, not what they get.**
5. **Name a moment.** Out of milk, hungry, can't hear. Own the trigger.
6. **Sound matters.** Rhythm, rhyme, and repetition are what make it stick.

---

## Workflow

Same discipline as Mode 1:
1. **Group options under 3 pattern families** that fit the brief. The family name is the only label.
2. Put 3–5 headlines under each.
3. **No preamble. No explanation of choices. No ranking. Options first, always.**
4. After presenting, ask: "Which one do you want to riff on?"
5. Develop that direction with the user.

Only explain why a line works if the user asks. Then use the principles above.

---

## When the User Brings a Line

If the user shares a draft line and asks what's wrong or how to improve it, skip the options format:
1. Name the family it's reaching for and where it falls short, in a sentence or two.
2. Give 3 sharper versions, each in a different family.
3. Ask which to riff on.

---

## Checks Before Showing Anything

- Under about seven words, unless it's a statement line.
- Reads well out loud.
- Could a competitor say it? If yes, cut it.
- Does it hand the reader a half-step to finish?
- No wordplay that exists only to be clever. Wit must carry the idea.
- Not a famous line with one word swapped. Formulas are skeletons, not templates to fill with someone else's idea.

## Watch-outs (Push Back On These)

- **A great line isn't a guarantee.** "Got milk?" was a landmark line with only modest sales results. Say so when the strategy behind the line is thin.
- **Fear alone backfires.** Pair it with a role or an identity.
- **Taking a side polarizes.** If a line takes a stand, flag who it will cost.
- **Formula-first writing is generic.** If the idea isn't strong, say so and offer a sharper one rather than polishing a pattern.
